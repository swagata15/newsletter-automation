# Research notes: Is AI making us lonely?

All figures below were read directly from the primary sources (paper full text, report PDF) on 2026-11-05.

## [1] Fang et al. (2025), MIT Media Lab + OpenAI, randomized controlled trial
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473
Title: How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use: A Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Study
Authors: Cathy Mengying Fang, Auren R. Liu, Valdemar Danry, Eunhae Lee, Samantha W. T. Chan, Pat Pataranutaporn, Pattie Maes, Jason Phang, Michael Lampe, Lama Ahmad, Sandhini Agarwal
- Design: four-week randomized controlled experiment, n=981, >300k messages. Conditions: text, neutral voice, engaging voice x open-ended, non-personal, personal conversations.
- Outcomes: loneliness (ULS-8, scale 1-4), socialization with real people (0-5), emotional dependence on AI, problematic AI usage.
- Abstract (verbatim): "No significant effects were detected from experimental conditions, despite conversation analyses revealing differences in AI and human behavioral patterns across the conditions. Instead, participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more, regardless of assigned condition, showed consistently worse outcomes."
- Abstract (verbatim): "These findings raise deeper questions about how artificial companions may reshape the ways people seek, sustain, and substitute human connections."
- Usage: "On average, participants spent 5.32 minutes per day on OpenAI's ChatGPT". Lowest user averaged 1.01 minutes, highest 27.65 minutes per day.
- Minutes per day by mode: text 4.35, neutral voice 5.56, engaging voice 6.16.
- Minutes per day by conversation type: open-ended 6.02, non-personal 4.92, personal 5.12.
- Results: across conditions loneliness fell slightly over four weeks, but participants also socialized significantly less with real people. No control group without a chatbot, so the authors note the change "could be attributed to other external factors, like holidays and seasonal changes".
- "participants who spent more daily time were significantly lonelier ... and socialized significantly less with real people" (p < 0.0001), and showed higher emotional dependence and problematic use.
- Controlling for time spent, voice modes were associated with lower loneliness than text.
- "personal topics slightly increased loneliness but tended to lower emotional dependence compared with open-ended conversations"
- "those with stronger emotional attachment tendencies and higher trust in the AI chatbot tended to experience greater loneliness"

## [2] Phang et al. (2025), OpenAI + MIT Media Lab, platform analysis
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03888
Title: Investigating Affective Use and Emotional Well-being on ChatGPT
- Analyzed over 3 million conversations for affective cues and surveyed over 4,000 users; parallel RCT of close to 1,000 participants over 28 days.
- Abstract (verbatim): "In both on-platform data analysis and the RCT, we observe that very high usage correlates with increased self-reported indicators of dependence."
- Abstract (verbatim): "Overall, our analysis reveals that a small number of users are responsible for a disproportionate share of the most affective cues."

## [3] Common Sense Media (2025), Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs: How and Why Teens Use AI Companions
URL: https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/talk-trust-and-trade-offs-how-and-why-teens-use-ai-companions
- Nationally representative survey of 1,060 US teens aged 13 to 17 (April to May 2025). Published July 16, 2025.
- 72% of teens have used AI companions at least once; 52% are regular users (a few times a month or more).
- Daily users are 13% of all teens (8% several times daily, 5% once daily); 21% use them a few times per week.
- 28% have never used an AI companion.
- Why teens use them (users): entertaining 30%, curious about the technology 28%, gives advice 18%, always available when I need someone to talk to 17%, nonjudgmental 14%.
- 31% find conversations with AI companions as satisfying or more satisfying than with real-life friends (21% about the same, 10% more satisfying). 67% find them less satisfying (47% much less, 20% somewhat less).
- 80% of users spend more time with real friends than with AI companions; 6% spend more time with AI companions.
- 33% of users have chosen to discuss important or serious matters with AI companions instead of real people (17% once or twice, 12% occasionally, 4% frequently).
- 39% of users have transferred social skills practiced with AI to real life (girls 45%, boys 34%).
- 34% of users report feeling uncomfortable with something an AI companion said or did.
- 24% of users have shared personal or private information with AI companions.
- 50% of teens distrust information or advice from AI companions; 23% trust them "quite a bit" or "completely".

## [4] De Freitas, Uguralp, Uguralp & Puntoni (2024), AI Companions Reduce Loneliness, Harvard Business School working paper
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19096
- Abstract (verbatim): "Study 3 finds that AI companions successfully alleviate loneliness on par only with interacting with another person, and more than other activities such watching YouTube videos. Moreover, consumers underestimate the degree to which AI companions improve their loneliness."
- "Study 4 uses a longitudinal design and finds that an AI companion consistently reduces loneliness over the course of a week."
- "Study 5 provides evidence that both the chatbots' performance and, especially, whether it makes users feel heard, explain reductions in loneliness."

## [5] Maples, Cerit, Vishwanath & Pea (2024), Loneliness and suicide mitigation for students using GPT3-enabled chatbots, npj Mental Health Research 3, 4
URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s44184-023-00047-6
- Survey of 1006 student users of Replika (18+, used it for over a month).
- "90% of the participant population experienced loneliness, and 43% qualified as Severely or Very Severely Lonely on the Loneliness Scale."
- Comparison: "approximately 53% of college students in the USA report loneliness" in prior studies.
- "90% also perceived medium to high social support"
- "3% reported that Replika halted their suicidal ideation" (30 participants).
- Stimulation vs displacement of human interaction: Comparison Group 23% stimulation, 8% displacement; Selected Group 37% stimulation, 13% displacement.
- A Matters Arising critique of the article was published 17 November 2025; self-selected survey, no control group.
